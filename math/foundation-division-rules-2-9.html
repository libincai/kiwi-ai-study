Division Rules (2–9)

These rules help us find out when a number can be divided exactly — with no remainder — and also explain why each rule works.

Below are some quick tests to check whether a number can be divided evenly by 2–9.

Each rule includes a simple reason that makes sense using place value.

Divisible by 2

Rule: The last digit is even (0, 2, 4, 6, 8).

Why: Every ten is 2 × 5, so tens, hundreds, … are all divisible by 2 already. Only the ones digit can cause a remainder — if it’s even, no remainder.

Examples: 2, 4, 6, 8, 22, 568, 5678

Divisible by 3

Rule: The sum of the digits is divisible by 3.

Why: Multiples of 10 can be written as a multiple of 9 plus 1:

10=9+1,100=99+1,1000=999+110 = 9 + 1,\quad 100 = 99 + 1,\quad 1000 = 999 + 1

For example:

456=4×100+5×10+6456 = 4 \times 100 + 5 \times 10 + 6

Rewrite 100 and 10:

456=4(99+1)+5(9+1)+6456 = 4(99 + 1) + 5(9 + 1) + 6

Expand and regroup:

456=(4×99+5×9)+(4+5+6)456 = (4 \times 99 + 5 \times 9) + (4 + 5 + 6)

The first part, 4×99+5×94 \times 99 + 5 \times 9, is divisible by 3. Therefore, we only need to check whether the sum of the digits is divisible by 3.

4+5+6=154 + 5 + 6 = 15

Since 15÷3=515 \div 3 = 5\ exactly, 456 is divisible by 3.

Key idea: Each place value contributes a multiple of 3 plus the digit itself. The multiples of 3 do not affect divisibility by 3, so we only need to check the sum of the digits.

Divisible by 4

Rule: The last two digits form a number divisible by 4.

Why: 100, 200, 300, and so on are all divisible by 4. Therefore, the hundreds, thousands, and higher place values are already divisible by 4. Only the last two digits determine whether the whole number is divisible by 4.

Example:

3,216÷43,216 \div 4 is exact because the last two digits are 16, and 16÷4=416 \div 4 = 4.

Divisible by 5

Rule: The last digit is 0 or 5.

Why: Every ten ends with 0 and is 5 × 2. Only a last digit of 0 or 5 fits into groups of 5.

Example:

975÷5975 \div 5 is exact (last digit 5)

Divisible by 6

Rule: Divisible by both 2 and 3.

Why: 6 = 2 × 3. If a number makes even groups (rule 2) and its digit-sum is a multiple of 3 (rule 3), then it makes groups of 6 with no remainder.

Examples:

126÷6126 \div 6 is exact because:

  • Rule 2: The last digit is 6, which is even.

  • Rule 3: 1+2+6=91 + 2 + 6 = 9, and 99 is divisible by 3.

Therefore,126\ 126 is divisible by 6.

Divisible by 7

Rule (subtract double-the-last-digit): Take the last digit, double it, and subtract from the other part. Repeat if needed. If you reach a small multiple of 7 (… −14, −7, 0, 7, 14, …), it’s divisible by 7.

Why:

Step 1: Split the number using place value.

Let NN\ be any whole number:

N=10a+bN = 10a + b

Here, aa is the number without the last digit, and bb is the last digit.

For example, for 301301, a=30a = 30 and b=1b = 1.

Since 10=7+310 = 7 + 3,

N=10a+b=7a+3a+bN = 10a + b = 7a + 3a + b

Because 7a7a\ is divisible by 7, we only need to check whether 3a+b3a + b is divisible by 7.

Step 2: Rewrite 3a+b3a + b.

Because 7b7b is divisible by 7, we want to rewrite 3a+bas3a + b\ as

3a+b=3a+7b−6b=3a−6b+7b=3(a−2b)+7b\begin{aligned} 3a + b & = 3a + 7b - 6b \\ & = 3a - 6b + 7b \\ & = 3(a - 2b) + 7b \end{aligned}

Therefore, we only need to check whether 3(a−2b)3(a - 2b) is divisible by 7.

Thus, we get the rule:

a−2b\boxed{a - 2b}

Double the last digit bb, then subtract it from the remaining number aa. If the result is divisible by 7, the original number is divisible by 7.

In simple words: Remove the last digit, double it, and subtract it from the remaining number. If the result is divisible by 7, the original number is also divisible by 7.

Example:

  • 301→30−2×1=28301 \rightarrow 30 - 2 \times 1 = 28

28=7×428 = 7 \times 4. So, 301 is divisible by 7.

  • 1,036→103−2×6=911,036 \rightarrow 103 - 2 \times 6 = 91

91=7×1391 = 7 \times 13. So, 1,036 is divisible by 7.

  • 1,204→120−2(4)=1121,204 \rightarrow 120 - 2(4) = 112

112→11−2(2)=7112 \rightarrow 11 - 2(2) = 7

So, 1,204 is divisible by 7.

Divisible by 8

Rule: The last three digits form a number divisible by 8.

Why: 1000, 2000, … are all multiples of 8 (since 1000 = 8 × 125). So only the last three digits can affect divisibility by 8.

Example:

7,360÷87,360 \div 8 is exact because the last three digits are 360, and 360÷8=45360 \div 8 = 45.

Divisible by 9

Rule: The sum of the digits is divisible by 9.

Why: 10, 100, 1000 … act like 1 for division by 9 (10 = 9 + 1, 100 = 99 + 1). So, the digit sum tells you everything.

Example:

459÷9459 \div 9 is exact because:

4+5+9=184 + 5 + 9 = 18

Since18÷9=2\ 18 \div 9 = 2, 459 is divisible by 9.
