Greatest Common Factor (GCF)

The Greatest Common Factor (GCF) of two or more whole numbers is the largest number that divides all of them without leaving a remainder.

Examples:

  • GCF(8,12)=4
  • GCF(18,24)=6
  • GCF(20,50)=10

In higher mathematics, the same idea is called the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD). Both mean the exact same thing.

In the examples below, you will see GCD inside math formula, even though we use GCF in the text for learning.

💡

This lesson focuses on advanced GCF methods — the subtraction method and Euclidean algorithm. For beginner-friendly methods — listing factors, prime factorization, and checking from the smaller number — see Basic Skills in Math Foundations.

Advanced Skills

The basic methods work well for small numbers, but they become slow or impractical when the numbers get large.

Examples:

  • Listing all factors of numbers like 840 and 1260 would take a long time.
  • Prime factorization becomes difficult when numbers are big or have many factors.
  • Checking factors one by one is not efficient for larger values.

To handle bigger numbers quickly, mathematicians use faster and more powerful methods such as the Subtraction Method and the Division Method (Euclidean Algorithm). These advanced skills help you find the GCF without listing dozens of factors or building large factor trees.

Subtraction Method

This method uses the idea (gcd\gcd is GCF):

gcd(a,b)=gcd(a−b,b)

Keep subtracting the smaller number from the larger one until both numbers match.

Example:

Find GCF(48, 18).

  1. 48−18=30 → (30, 18)
  2. 30−18=12 → (18, 12)
  3. 18−12=6 → (12, 6)
  4. 12−6=6 → both numbers match

∴ GCF(48, 18) = 6.

💡History of the Subtraction Method

The subtraction method for finding the GCF is actually very old — it appeared in both ancient China and ancient Greece, developed independently.

  • In ancient China, the idea shows up in the classic math text 《九章算术》 (The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art), written around 200 BCE to 100 CE. Chinese mathematicians used repeated subtraction to simplify ratios and find common measures — exactly the same idea as gcd(a,b)=gcd(a−b,b)\gcd(a,b) = gcd(a - b,b).
  • In ancient Greece, around the same time (300 BCE), the mathematician Euclid described the same method in Elements, Book VII. Because division was difficult back then, repeated subtraction was the easiest way to find the greatest common measure.

So, the simple subtraction method has a long history — used by early mathematicians in both China and Greece more than 2,000 years ago!

Division Method (Euclidean Algorithm)

This method is also called Euclidean Algorithm. It is the fastest method for large numbers.

The Subtraction Method works, but it becomes slow when the numbers are far apart.

For example, to find GCF(84, 30) using subtraction:

84−30=54

54−30=24

30−24=6

24−6=18

18−6=12

12−6=6

You must keep subtracting many times to reach the answer.

If the numbers were even larger — like 840 and 126 — this method would take many more steps.

Because subtraction only reduces the number little by little, mathematicians created a faster method: division.

Division jumps straight to the remainder in one step: 84÷30=2remainder2484 \div 30 = 2\ remainder\ 24.

This reduces the problem immediately from (84, 30) to (30, 24), skipping all the repeated subtraction.

Therefore, the Division Method is the quickest and most powerful way to find the GCF, especially for large numbers.

Rule:

gcd(a,b)=gcd(b,r)\gcd(a,b) = gcd(b,r)

where r is the remainder when a is divided by b. Repeat this process until the remainder becomes 0. gcd\gcd is GCF.

Example:

Find GCF(84, 30).

  1. 84 ÷ 30 = 2 remainder 24
  2. 30 ÷ 24 = 1 remainder 6
  3. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 remainder 0

When the remainder is 0, the last divisor is the GCF.

∴ GCF(84, 30) = 6.

💡History of the Division Method

The division method for finding the GCF is a faster version of the ancient subtraction method .

  • Around 300 BCE, the Greek mathematician Euclid discovered that you could jump straight to the answer by using division. He wrote this idea in Elements, Book VII: gcd(a,b)=gcd(b,;abmodb)\gcd(a,b) = gcd(b,;ab\ mod\ b).
  • In ancient China, mathematicians also used division to simplify ratios and fractions, especially in 《九章算术》. The logic behind it is the same idea of reducing numbers using division.
  • Over time, Euclid’s division method became known as the Euclidean Algorithm. Modern computers still use it today because it is extremely fast and efficient.

So, the division method you learn in class is not new — it is a powerful idea that has been used for more than 2,000 years, and it is still the fastest way to find the GCF!

Why Does the Division Method Work?

When we divide aa\ by bb, we can write:

a=bq+ra = bq + r

where qq is the quotient and rr is the remainder.

Rearranging gives:

r=a−bqr = a - bq

Any number that divides both aa and bb must also divide rr, because r=a−bqr = a - bq.

Similarly, any number that divides both bb and rr must also divide aa, because a=bq+ra = bq + r.

Therefore, aa and bb have exactly the same common factors as bb and rr. In particular,

GCF(a,b)=GCF(b,r)\boxed{{GCF}(a,b) = {GCF}(b,r)}

We can repeat this process, replacing the larger number with the remainder each time. When the remainder becomes zero, the last non-zero remainder is the GCF.
