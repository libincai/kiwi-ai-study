Number Strategies

Use mathematical properties to simplify numerical calculations.

Ontario Curriculum Connection

Ontario Curriculum Connection

Grade 3 Mathematics — B2.1: Properties and Relationships

The Ontario curriculum expects students to use the properties of operations and the relationships between multiplication and division to solve problems and check calculations.

How This Topic Connects

This topic develops students’ understanding of the commutative, associative, and distributive properties, as well as inverse operations. Students learn how these properties can be used to simplify calculations and check their answers.

Official source: https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum/elementary-mathematics/grades/g3-math/strand-b/b2

Grade 9 Mathematics — C1.4: Simplifying Algebraic Expressions

The Ontario curriculum expects students to simplify algebraic expressions by applying the properties of operations of numbers.

How This Topic Connects

The same mathematical properties used to simplify numerical calculations also form the foundation for simplifying algebraic expressions. Understanding these properties helps students recognize equivalent expressions and apply familiar strategies to more advanced mathematics.

Official source: https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum/secondary-mathematics/courses/mth1w/c/c1

Commutative Property

Order doesn’t matter. Swap numbers’ positions.

Addition: a+b=b+aa + b = b + a

Example:

3+4=4+3=73 + 4 = 4 + 3 = 7

Multiplication: a×b=b×aa \times b = b \times a

Example:

3×4=4×3=123 \times 4 = 4 \times 3 = 12

Associative property

Grouping can change. Change who’s grouped together (or the order of operation).

Addition: (a+b)+c=a+(b+c)(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)

Example:

18+49+32=(18+32)+49=50+49=99\begin{aligned} 18 + 49 + 32 \\ & = (18 + 32) + 49 \\ & = 50 + 49 \\ & = 99 \end{aligned}

Multiplication: (a×b)×c=a×(b×c)(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)

Example:

3×25×4=3×(25×4)=3×100=300\begin{aligned} 3 \times 25 \times 4 \\ & = 3 \times (25 \times 4) \\ & = 3 \times 100 \\ & = 300 \end{aligned}

Distributive property

Times spreads over plus or minus

Over addition: a(b+c)=a×b+a×ca(b + c) = a \times b + a \times c

Example:21×(100+2)=21×100+21×2=2100+42=2142\begin{aligned} 21 \times (100 + 2) \\ & = 21 \times 100 + 21 \times 2 \\ & = 2100 + 42 \\ & = 2142 \end{aligned}

Over subtraction: a(b−c)=a×b−a×ca(b - c) = a \times b - a \times c

Example:

21×(100−2)=21×100−21×2=2100−42=2058\begin{aligned} 21 \times (100 - 2) \\ & = 21 \times 100 - 21 \times 2 \\ & = 2100 - 42 \\ & = 2058 \end{aligned}

Factoring — Reverse Distributive

Pull out the common factor

From a sum: a×b+a×c=a×(b+c)a \times b + a \times c = a \times (b + c)

Example:

31×99+31×1=31×(99+1)=31×100=3100\begin{aligned} 31 \times 99 + 31 \times 1 \\ = & 31 \times (99 + 1) \\ = & 31 \times 100 \\ = & 3100 \end{aligned}

From a difference: a×b−a×c=a×(b−c)a \times b - a \times c = a \times (b - c)

Example:

48×101−48=48×(101−1)=48×100=4800\begin{aligned} 48 \times 101 - 48 \\ & = 48 \times (101 - 1) \\ & = 48 \times 100 \\ & = 4800 \end{aligned}

Note: 48=48×148 = 48 \times 1

Mixed commutative and associative properties

Commutative → You swap numbers’ positions.

Associative → You change who’s grouped together (or the order of operation).

Both → You do both swapping and regrouping.

Example:

5.76+13.67+4.24+6.33=(5.76+4.24)+(13.67+6.33)=10+20=30\begin{aligned} 5.76 + 13.67 + 4.24 + 6.33 \\ & = (5.76 + 4.24) + (13.67 + 6.33) \\ & = 10 + 20 \\ & = 30 \end{aligned}

Near multiples of 10

Examples:

  • Addition:

97+76=(100−3)+76=100+76−3=176−3=173\begin{aligned} 97 + 76 \\ & = (100 - 3) + 76 \\ & = 100 + 76 - 3 \\ & = 176 - 3 \\ & = 173 \end{aligned}

  • Subtraction:

1000−498=1000−(500−2)=(1000−500)+2=502\begin{aligned} 1000 - 498 \\ & = 1000 - (500 - 2) \\ & = (1000 - 500) + 2 \\ & = 502 \end{aligned}

  • Multiplication:

35×9.9=35×(10−0.1)=35×10−35×0.1=350−3.5=346.5\begin{aligned} 35 \times 9.9 \\ & = 35 \times (10 - 0.1) \\ & = 35 \times 10 - 35 \times 0.1 \\ & = 350 - 3.5 \\ & = 346.5 \end{aligned}

57×101=57×(100+1)=57×100+57×1=5700+57=5,757\begin{aligned} 57 \times 101 \\ & = 57 \times (100 + 1) \\ & = 57 \times 100 + 57 \times 1 \\ & = 5700 + 57 \\ & = 5,757 \end{aligned}

Simplifying multiplication and division

Multiply 5 Using Friend Number

To multiply any number by 5, use 5’s 10-friend — the number 2.

Steps:

  1. Add a 0 to the end of the number (this makes it ×10\times 10).

  2. Divide the new number by 2.

  3. The result is the same as multiplying by 5!

Examples:

  • 266×5

    • 266 → Add 0 → 2,660

    • 2,660 → Divide by 2 → 1,330

  • 1,217×5

    • 1,217 → Add 0 → 12,170

    • 12,170 → Divide by 2 → 6,085 Using Divide by 2 Using Easy Splits method:

      • Split 12,170 → 12, 170

      • (÷2) → 6, 085

Divide by 2 Using Easy Splits

When you divide a number by 2, you can make the work much easier by splitting the number into small parts. Try to make each part 1–2 digits. If that’s not possible, use 3 digits.

Then simply divide each part by 2 and write the answers together.

If the final answer has fewer digits than the original number, add zeros at the front to keep the place value correct.

Examples:

  • 2,638÷2:

    • Split 2,638 into 26 and 38.

    • 26 ÷ 2 = 13

    • 38 ÷ 2 = 19

      So the answer is 1,319.

  • 32,108÷2:

    • Split 32,108 into 32, 10, and 8.

    • 32 ÷ 2 = 16

    • 10 ÷ 2 = 5

    • 8 ÷ 2 = 4

      Pad with a zero to keep the place value → 16054.

Divide Using Fractions and Friendly Numbers

When a division problem is hard to compute directly, turn it into a fraction. Once it becomes a fraction, you can make the numbers easier in two ways.

Method 1: Use a Friend Number

Find a number that turns the denominator into a power of 10 (like 100 or 1000). Multiply both the top and bottom by that friend number to make the division simple.

Examples:

  • 7÷25:

    • Conver to fraction:

7÷25→7257 \div 25 \rightarrow \frac{7}{25}

  • 25’s 100-friend is 4:

7×425×4=28100=0.28\frac{7 \times 4}{25 \times 4}\ = \frac{28}{100} = 0.28

  • 7÷125:

    • Conver to fraction:

7÷125→71257 \div 125 \rightarrow \frac{7}{125}

  • 125’s 1000-friend is 8:

7×8125×8=561000=0.056\frac{7 \times 8}{125 \times 8} = \frac{56}{1000} = 0.056

Method 2: Reduce the Fraction

If the numbers share a common factor, divide both the top and bottom until they become small.

Example:

126÷36:

  • Conver to fraction:

126÷36→12636126 \div 36 \rightarrow \frac{126}{36}

  • Divide top and bottom by 6:

126÷636÷6=216\frac{126 \div 6}{36 \div 6} = \frac{21}{6}

  • Divide again by 3:

21÷36÷3=72=3.5\frac{21 \div 3}{6 \div 3} = \frac{7}{2} = 3.5

Pairing Trick for Sums

Ontario Curriculum Connection

Ontario Curriculum Connection

Grade 4 Mathematics — C1: Patterns and Relationships

The Ontario curriculum expects students to identify, describe, extend, and create patterns, and to determine pattern rules that can be used to make predictions.

How This Topic Connects

Gauss’s Pairing Trick helps students recognize a numerical pattern by pairing the first and last numbers, the second and second-last numbers, and so on. Each pair has the same sum, allowing students to replace repeated addition with multiplication.

Official source: https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum/elementary-mathematics/grades/g4-math/strand-c/c1

Grade 9 Mathematics — C1.2: Generalizing Relationships

The Ontario curriculum expects students to create algebraic expressions that generalize relationships expressed in words, numbers, and visual representations.

How This Topic Connects

Building on the pairing pattern, students can generalize the method to the sum of the first (n) natural numbers and derive the formula:
1+2+3+⋯+n=n(n+1)21 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}

This activity connects numerical patterns with algebraic generalization and demonstrates how a specific calculation can lead to a formula that works for any positive integer (n).

Official source: https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum/secondary-mathematics/courses/mth1w/c/c1

Sometimes we don’t want to add one number at a time. We want a smart way.

A famous story: when the young mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss was in school, the teacher asked the class to add

1+2+3+…+1001 + 2 + 3 + \ldots + 100

The teacher thought it would take a long time. But Gauss finished in seconds. How?

Gauss’s Pairing Idea

Write the sum forwards and backwards:

1+2+3+…+99+1001 + 2 + 3 + \ldots + 99 + 100

100+99+98+…+2+1100 + 99 + 98 + \ldots + 2 + 1

Pair them in columns:

(1+100)+(2+99)+(3+98)+…+(99+2)+(100+1)(1 + 100) + (2 + 99) + (3 + 98) + \ldots + (99 + 2) + (100 + 1)

Each pair adds to the same value:

1+100=1011 + 100 = 101

2+99=1012 + 99 = 101

3+98=1013 + 98 = 101

There are 100 numbers, which make 50 pairs, and each pair is 101:

1+2+3+…+100=50×101=50501 + 2 + 3 + \ldots + 100 = 50 \times 101 = 5050

General Rule

The same idea works for

1+2+3+…+n1 + 2 + 3 + \ldots + n

  • First number: 1, and last number: 𝑛

  • Reverse the numbers in a second row and align as

(1,n),(2,n−1),…,(n,1)(1,n),(2,n - 1),\ldots,(n,1)

  • Each pair adds to 𝑛+1.

  • There are nn numbers in the original sequence. When we write the sequence forwards and backwards in two rows, we have 2n2n numbers, forming nn pairs. Each pair adds up to (n+1). Therefore, the total of both rows is

n(n+1)n(n + 1)

  • Divide by 2 (because we added the sequence twice):

n(n+1)2\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}

So, the formula is:

1+2+3+…+n=n(n+1)21 + 2 + 3 + \ldots + n = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}\
