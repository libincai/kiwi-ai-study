Basic Geometry Words

Point

A point is a specific location in space. It has no size (no length, width, or height) and is usually represented by a dot and a capital letter.

Examples:

  • 𝐴 (PointAPoint\ A)
  • A dot on a map marking a city is a good visualization of a point.
Line

A line is a perfectly straight, continuous path that extends infinitely in both directions. It has no thickness and is typically named by two points on it or a single lowercase letter.

Example:

AB↔\overset{\leftrightarrow}{AB}

A line may also be named using a lowercase letter, such as 𝓁\mathcal{l}.

Example:

Line 𝓁\text{Line~}\mathcal{l}

Line Segment

A line segment is a part of a line with two distinct endpoints. It has a definite length.

Examples:

  • AB (Line segment AB)

AB¯\overline{AB}

  • A ruler or the edge of a piece of paper are examples of line segments.
Ray

A ray is a part of a line that has one endpoint and extends infinitely in only one direction.

Examples:

  • A⟶B (Ray AB, starting at A and going through B)

AB→\overset{\rightarrow}{AB}

  • A⟵B (Ray BA, starting at B and going through A)

BA→\overset{\rightarrow}{BA}

  • A beam of light from a flashlight or the sun’s rays are good visualizations of a ray.
Angle

An angle is formed when two rays share a common endpoint (the vertex). Angles are measured in degrees.

Examples:

  • Acute angle: Measures less than 90°.
  • Right angle: Measures exactly 90°. It often has a small square drawn in the corner to indicate this.
  • Obtuse angle: Measures greater than 90° but less than 180°.
  • Straight angle: Measures exactly 180°. It forms a straight line.
  • Reflex angle: Measures greater than 180° but less than 360°.
Parallel And Perpendicular Lines

Parallel lines

Parallel lines are two lines in a plane that are always the same distance apart and never intersect, no matter how far they are extended.

Notation:

𝓁∥m\mathcal{l \parallel}m

Examples:

  • Noted using the symbol // (e.g., Line A // Line B).
  • Train tracks
  • Opposite edges of a ruler

Perpendicular lines

Perpendicular lines are two lines that intersect (cross) to form a right angle of exactly 90∘90^{\circ}.

Notation:

𝓁⊥m\mathcal{l\bot}m

Examples:

  • Noted using the symbol ⟂ (e.g., Line C ⟂ Line D).
  • The corners of a room where the wall meets the floor

Vertex

(plural: vertices)

A vertex is a corner point where two or more lines, rays, or edges meet.

Examples:

  • The shared endpoint where the two rays of an angle meet is the vertex.
  • The corners of a square, triangle, or cube are all vertices.
Polygons and Shapes

Polygon

A polygon is a closed, flat (2D) shape made up of three or more straight line segments joined end-to-end. The segments only meet at their endpoints (vertices).

Examples:

  • Triangles, squares, rectangles, hexagons, and octagons are all polygons.
  • Circles and shapes with curved sides are not polygons.

Triangle

A triangle is a polygon with exactly three sides and three angles. Triangles are classified by their side lengths and angles:

Examples:

  • Equilateral: All 3 sides are equal in length, and all 3 angles are 60°.
  • Isosceles: Exactly 2 sides are equal in length, and the two angles opposite those sides are equal.
  • Scalene: All 3 sides have different lengths, and all 3 angles are different measures.
  • Right triangle: One of the interior angles measures exactly 90°. The side opposite the right angle is called the hypotenuse.

Quadrilateral

A quadrilateral is a polygon with exactly four sides and four angles. Specific quadrilaterals have special properties:

Examples:

  1. Square: 4 equal sides and 4 right angles.
  • Rectangle: 4 right angles and opposite sides are equal in length.
  • Parallelogram: Opposite sides are parallel and equal in length.
  • Rhombus: 4 equal sides (it’s a slanted square).
  • Trapezoid: At least one pair of opposite sides are parallel.

Regular polygon

A regular polygon is a polygon that is both equilateral (all sides have the same length) and equiangular (all interior angles have the same measure).

Examples:

  • An equilateral triangle is a regular polygon.
  • A square is a regular polygon.
  • A rhombus is not a regular polygon unless it is also a square (angles are not always equal).

Circle terms

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are a fixed distance (radius) from a central point.

Examples:

  • Radius (𝑟): The distance from the exact center of the circle to any point on its edge.
  • Diameter (𝑑): The distance across the circle through the center. It is always twice the radius (𝑑=2𝑟).
  • Circumference (𝐶): The total distance around the outside of the circle (the perimeter).
  • Chord: A straight line segment whose endpoints both lie on the circle. (The diameter is the longest chord).
  • Arc: A portion or section of the curve that makes up the circle’s circumference.
  • Pi (𝑝𝑖): A mathematical constant, approximately 3.14159…, representing the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter

Measurement

Perimeter

The perimeter is the total distance around the outside edge of a 2D shape. You calculate it by adding up the lengths of all the sides.

Example:

The perimeter of a rectangle with length 5cm and width 3cm is 5+3+5+3=165 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16 (cm).

Surface area

Surface area is the total area of all the outside surfaces (faces) of a 3D object. You imagine unfolding the shape into a flat “net” and finding the area of every single piece, then adding them up.

Examples:

  • Prisms:

Find the area of the front, back, top, bottom, left, and right faces, then add them all together.

  • Cylinders:

The surface area is the area of the top and bottom circles plus the area of the rectangular side (when unrolled).

Net

A net is a 2D (flat) shape that can be folded along its edges to form a 3D solid figure. It’s like the cardboard layout of a box before it’s assembled.

Example:

A net of a cube consists of six squares connected in a cross shape.

3D figures

Prism

A prism is a 3D solid shape with two identical, parallel bases and rectangular sides (lateral faces) connecting them. Prisms are named by the shape of their base.

Examples:

  • Rectangular Prism: Has rectangular bases (like a cereal box). Volume is calculated as:

V=length×width×heightV = length \times width \times height

  • Triangular Prism: Has triangular bases.

Pyramid

A pyramid is a 3D shape where the base is a polygon (e.g., square, triangle) and all the sides are triangles that meet at a single point at the top called the apex.

Example:

A square pyramid has a square base and four triangular sides.

Cylinder

A cylinder is a 3D shape with two parallel, congruent circular bases and one curved side.

Example:

A can of soup or a roll of paper towel.

Cone

A cone is a 3D shape that has a flat, circular base and one curved side that tapers up to a single point (apex).

Example:

An ice cream cone or a traffic cone.

Sphere

A sphere is a perfectly round 3D object where every point on its surface is the same distance from its center. It is like a 3D circle.

Example:

A basketball, a marble, or Earth.

Cube

A cube is a special type of rectangular prism where all 6 faces are identical squares.

Example:

A standard die (dice) or a Rubik’s cube.

Transformations

Translation

A translation is a transformation that “slides” a shape from one position to another without changing its size, shape, or orientation.

Example:

If you slide a triangle up 3 units and to the right 2 units, you are performing a translation.

Reflection

A reflection is a transformation that “flips” a shape over a line (the line of reflection), creating a mirror image.

Example:

If you flip a triangle over the y-axis, its new position is a reflection of the original.

Rotation

A rotation is a transformation that “turns” a shape around a fixed point (the center of rotation). The shape maintains its size and form.

Example:

A rotation can be clockwise or counter-clockwise, for a specific number of degrees.

Dilation

A dilation is a transformation that changes the size of a shape but not its form. It either enlarges or shrinks the figure based on a scale factor from a central point.

Example:

If a shape is dilated by a scale factor of 2, its new dimensions will be twice as large.

Congruent figures

Congruent figures are shapes that have the exact same size and shape. One can be perfectly mapped onto the other using a combination of translations, reflections, and rotations.

Examples:

  • Squares: Two squares are congruent if all four corresponding sides have the same length and all four corresponding angles measure 90∘90^{\circ}.
  • Triangles: Two triangles are congruent if their corresponding side lengths and corresponding angle measures are equal.
  • Transformations: A shape and its rotated, reflected, or translated image are congruent because these transformations preserve size and shape.

Similar figures

Similar figures are shapes that have the same form but not necessarily the same size. Their corresponding angles are equal, and their corresponding sides are proportional.

Examples:

  • A small square and a large square are similar figures.
  • A picture and a scaled-down version of it are similar.

Line of symmetry

A line of symmetry is a line that divides a figure into two identical halves. If you fold the figure along this line, both halves will match up perfectly.

Examples:

  • An isosceles triangle has one line of symmetry.
  • A square has four lines of symmetry.
Position

Coordinate plane

A coordinate plane is a flat, 2D surface formed by two intersecting number lines, the x-axis and the y-axis, used to locate points.

Example:

Also called a Cartesian plane or a coordinate grid.

X-axis, y-axis

  • x-axis: The horizontal number line on the coordinate plane.
  • y-axis: The vertical number line on the coordinate plane.

Example:

The x-axis measures horizontal distance, and the y-axis measures vertical distance.

Origin

The origin is the point where the x-axis and the y-axis intersect. It is located at the coordinates (0,0)(0,0).

Example:

The starting point for all measurements on the coordinate plane.

Ordered pair

An ordered pair is a set of two numbers, (𝑥,𝑦), that represent a single point’s location on the coordinate

plane. The first number is the x-coordinate, and the second is the y-coordinate.

Example:

To find the point (3, −2), you move 3 units right on the x-axis and 2 units down on the y-axis.

Quadrants

The four regions on the coordinate plane created by the intersection of the x-axis and the y-axis. They are numbered using Roman numerals, starting from the top right and moving counterclockwise.

Examples:

  • Quadrant I: Positive x and y coordinates e.g., (3,5).
  • Quadrant II: Negative x and positive y coordinates e.g., (−2,4).
  • Quadrant III: Negative x and y coordinates e.g., (−1,−6).
  • Quadrant IV: Positive x and negative y coordinates e.g., (7,−2).
