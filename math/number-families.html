Meet the Number Families

Start with the numbers we count with, then explore numbers that describe parts, values below zero, and ideas beyond the ordinary number line.

1. Natural Numbers

The counting numbers we use every day. They start at 1 and continue forever.

Examples: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, …

A little history
Long before written symbols, people counted with fingers, pebbles, and tally marks to keep track of animals and food. These counting ideas became the natural numbers.

2. Whole Numbers

Whole numbers include zero and all natural numbers.

Examples: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, …

A little history
Zero developed over time. Indian mathematicians gave zero a clear place in the number system; Brahmagupta described rules for zero around 628 CE. The idea later spread widely.

3. Integers

Integers include zero, positive whole numbers, and negative whole numbers.

Examples: …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …

A little history
Negative numbers once seemed strange. In the 7th century, Indian mathematician Brahmagupta described positive amounts as fortunes and negative amounts as debts. The symbol ℤ comes from the German word Zahlen, meaning numbers.

4. Fractions

Fractions show equal parts of a whole. The top number is the numerator; the bottom number is the denominator.

Examples: 1/2, 9/8, 3/8, 1 3/4

A little history
About 4,000 years ago, Egyptians used unit fractions to share food and measure land. Babylonians and Greeks also used fractions in trade and astronomy. Fraction notation gradually developed into the form we use today.

5. Decimals

Decimals are another way to write parts of a whole, using place value and a decimal point.

Examples: 0.5 = 1/2; 0.25 = 1/4

A little history
In 1585, Simon Stevin published a book explaining decimal fractions and how they could make calculations easier. Decimal notation became especially useful for money, measurement, and science.

6. Rational Numbers

A rational number can be written as a fraction of two integers, with a non-zero denominator. Its decimal ends or repeats.

Examples: 1/2, 3/4, 0.5, 0.333…

A little history
People have used ratios for thousands of years to compare lengths, share goods, and study the sky. Greek mathematicians explored ratios deeply, while many cultures developed practical fraction methods.

7. Irrational Numbers

Irrational numbers cannot be written as a fraction of two integers. Their decimals never end and never repeat in a pattern.

Examples: π = 3.14159…; √2 = 1.41421…

A little history
Around 500 BCE, Greek mathematicians discovered that the diagonal of a unit square, √2, cannot be expressed as a ratio of whole numbers. This surprising discovery changed how people thought about numbers.

8. Imaginary Numbers

Imaginary numbers involve the square root of a negative number. The basic imaginary unit is i, where i² = −1.

Example: √−1 = i

A little history
In the 1500s, mathematicians met square roots of negative numbers while solving equations. Euler later helped establish the symbol i, and Gauss helped develop the theory of complex numbers. These ideas are now useful in physics and engineering.

A helpful connection: natural numbers are part of the whole numbers, whole numbers are part of the integers, and integers are part of the rational numbers. Irrational numbers are real numbers too, while imaginary numbers help form the larger complex number system.

Number Basics: 0 and 1

These two small numbers have some very special properties.

Number 0

  • 0 is neither positive nor negative.
  • In this guide, 0 is not a natural number.
  • 0 is a whole number, an integer, and a rational number.
  • 0 is even because 0 ÷ 2 = 0 with no remainder.
  • Division by 0 is undefined; 0 cannot be a fraction's denominator.
  • 0.0 is a decimal representation of zero.

Number 1

  • 1 raised to any whole-number power is still 1: 1² = 1, 1⁵ = 1, 1¹⁰⁰ = 1.
  • 1 is neither prime nor composite.
  • A prime number has exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself. For example, 2 has factors 1 and 2.
  • A composite number has more than two positive factors. For example, 6 has 1, 2, 3, and 6.
  • 1 has only one positive factor — itself — so it belongs to neither group.

Note: Some textbooks include 0 among the natural numbers. Always check which convention your class or textbook uses.
