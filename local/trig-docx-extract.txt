
## Understanding Trigonometry

Trigonometry is the study of relationships between the angles and side lengths of triangles. It helps us find unknown sides and angles without measuring them directly.
Trigonometry is useful for calculating heights, distances, slopes, and angles in real-life situations.
In this chapter, you will learn how to:
Identify the sides of a right triangle.
Understand and use the four basic trigonometric ratios.
Derive special-angle values instead of memorizing them.
Find missing side lengths and angles.
Solve practical problems involving heights and distances.

## The Three Sides of a Right Triangle

A right triangle has one angle measuring $90∘$.
The three sides have different names depending on their positions relative to the reference angle $θ$.
Hypotenuse: The side opposite the right angle. It is always the longest side.
Opposite side: The side directly across from the reference angle $θ$.
Adjacent side: The side next to $θ$, excluding the hypotenuse.

Angles and Trigonometric Ratios
Angles in Standard Position

## Initial Arm and Terminal Arm

An angle is in standard position when its vertex is at the origin and its initial arm lies along the positive x-axis. Its terminal arm is the ray reached after rotating through the angle.
Counterclockwise rotation gives a positive angle; clockwise rotation gives a negative angle. Angles may be greater than 360° or negative.

## Primary Trigonometric Ratios on the Coordinate Plane

If P(x, y) lies on the terminal arm and r = √(x² + y²), then r is the distance from the origin to P.
On the unit circle r = 1, so the point on the terminal arm is P(cos θ, sin θ). The signs of the ratios follow the quadrant of P.

## Signs of Trigonometric Ratios in Four Quadrants

Quadrant I: sin +, cos +, tan +. Quadrant II: sin +, cos −, tan −. Quadrant III: sin −, cos −, tan +. Quadrant IV: sin −, cos +, tan −.
Related and Coterminal Angles

## Related Acute Angle (Reference Angle)

A related acute angle is the acute angle between the terminal arm and the x-axis. It is also called the reference angle. Its size is always between 0° and 90° for non-axis angles.
For angles outside 0°–360°, first find a coterminal angle in that interval.

## Coterminal Angles

Angles that have the same initial arm and terminal arm are coterminal. They differ by whole rotations of 360°.
Example: 480°, 120°, and −240° are coterminal. All three terminate in Quadrant II.

Reference Angles in All Four Quadrants
A reference angle is the smallest positive angle between the terminal side and the x-axis (positive or negative). Measure it from the nearest x-axis direction, not from the y-axis. For angles between 0° and 360°:

Angles on an axis (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°) are boundary cases; their reference angle is 0° or 90°, rather than an acute angle. For other angles, first find a coterminal angle between 0° and 360°.
Example: 210° (Quadrant III)
The nearest x-axis direction is 180°, so the reference angle is 210° − 180° = 30°. Both x and y are negative, making sine and cosine negative, but tangent and cotangent positive.
Example: 300° (Quadrant IV)
The nearest x-axis direction is 360°, so the reference angle is 360° − 300° = 60°. Here x is positive and y is negative.

How to Remember Trigonometric Signs: Draw the Signed Right Triangle
Instead of memorizing quadrant sign rules, draw a right triangle from the terminal side to the x-axis. Treat the horizontal and vertical legs as signed projections: left is negative x, right is positive x; up is positive y, down is negative y. The hypotenuse r is always a positive length.
Quadrant II: x < 0, y > 0, r > 0. Mark the horizontal leg negative and the vertical leg positive. Therefore sin is positive; cos, tan and cot are negative.
Quadrant III: x < 0, y < 0, r > 0. Mark both legs negative. Therefore sin and cos are negative, while tan and cot are positive (negative ÷ negative = positive).
Quadrant IV: x > 0, y < 0, r > 0. Mark the horizontal leg positive and the vertical leg negative. Therefore cos is positive; sin, tan and cot are negative.
To determine each sign, use the ratios sin θ = y/r, cos θ = x/r, tan θ = y/x and cot θ = x/y, wherever the denominator is nonzero. The signs follow from the numerator and denominator.
Teaching tip: The triangle side lengths themselves are never negative. The + and − marks represent the direction of the signed x- and y-projections, not negative physical lengths.

## Reciprocal Trigonometric Ratios

The reciprocal of sine is cosecant (csc), the reciprocal of cosine is secant (sec), and the reciprocal of tangent is cotangent (cot).
The ratios are undefined when their denominators are zero. Do not confuse reciprocal ratios with inverse trigonometric functions (arcsin, arccos, arctan).
Oblique Triangles and Triangle Laws
Oblique Triangles and the Sine Law

## Types of Oblique Triangles

An oblique triangle is any triangle that is not a right triangle. An acute triangle has all three angles smaller than 90°. An obtuse triangle has one angle greater than 90°.

## The Sine Law

For triangle ABC, side a is opposite angle A, side b is opposite angle B, and side c is opposite angle C. The Sine Law relates each side to the sine of its opposite angle.
Use the Sine Law when a known angle–opposite-side pair is available, for example ASA, AAS, or SSA. For SAS or SSS, the Law of Cosines is generally the starting point.

## The SSA Ambiguous Case

When two side lengths and a non-included angle are given (SSA), two distinct oblique triangles may satisfy the same data. This is called the ambiguous case. See the separate SSA Ambiguous Case section below for the full height test and diagram.

## Using the Sine Law in Applications

Sketch the situation, label each angle and its opposite side, check that the calculator is in degree mode, choose the matching side–angle ratio, solve, and check whether a second angle is possible.
For an angle found from sin B = k, the second candidate is 180° − B. Keep it only if the triangle’s angles still sum to less than 180° before finding the final angle.

## What Is the Law of Cosines?

The Law of Cosines solves general triangles, not only right triangles. Use two sides and the included angle to find the third side, or use three sides to find an angle. Let a, b, and c be the side lengths, with angle C opposite side c.
Four Quantities — Know Three, Find One
The four quantities are a, b, c, and C: three sides and one angle. In the standard cases below, three suitable known values determine the fourth.
Case 1 — Find a Side
Given a, b, and the included angle C, substitute directly to find c.
Case 2 — Find an Angle
Given a, b, and c, rearrange the equation to find C.
Learning strategy: Identify what is known and unknown before choosing the formula. Not every arbitrary set of three quantities determines a unique fourth value.
Remember Through Association
Students sometimes forget the −2ab cos C term, or mistakenly write −2ac cos C or −2bc cos C. Connect the formula to the square of a difference:
The Law of Cosines has the same pattern, with an extra cos C factor in the final term.
Why 2ab, Not 2ac or 2bc?
When finding c from known a, b, and C, the right-hand side must be computable using those known quantities. The cosine term therefore uses the other two sides, 2ab cos C. An unknown on both sides does not always make an equation wrong, but this is a useful check for this formula.

Applications in 3D Settings

## Working with Three Dimensions

Use the SOH–CAH–TOA ratios, the Sine Law, and the Law of Cosines in three-dimensional situations. Identify the horizontal plane, vertical height, and slanted segment before solving.
Draw a 3D sketch, then isolate a right triangle or an oblique triangle in a plane. Solve one triangle at a time and use its result in the next triangle.
Typical applications include the height of a regular tetrahedron, the length of a diagonal through a rectangular solid, and a distance involving height and horizontal separation.

## Choosing Between the Sine Law and the Cosine Law

Once you know both laws, identify the information given in the triangle before choosing a formula.
Reading the abbreviations:  S = Side (a known side length);  A = Angle (a known angle).  The letters describe the order of the given parts around the triangle: ASA = Angle–Side–Angle; AAS = Angle–Angle–Side; SSA = Side–Side–Angle; SAS = Side–Angle–Side; SSS = Side–Side–Side. In SAS, the angle lies between the two known sides; in SSA, the known angle is not between the two known sides.
Quick Tip: Pair → Sine; Sandwich → Cosine.
Sine Law: Look for a known angle and its opposite side (a complete opposite pair). This is usually the fastest starting point for ASA, AAS, or SSA. For SSA, check the ambiguous case: there may be zero, one, or two triangles.
Cosine Law: Use it when you know two sides and their included angle (SAS), or all three sides (SSS). An included angle is the angle between the two known sides.

Given ASA or AAS → Find the third angle using 180°, then use the Sine Law.
Given SSA → Start with the Sine Law; check for the ambiguous case.
Given SAS → Start with the Cosine Law to find the opposite side.
Given SSS → Start with the Cosine Law to find an angle.
To finish solving a triangle, you may switch between the two laws. Check that the larger side is opposite the larger angle.

What Is an Identity?
A trigonometric identity is an equation that is true for every angle for which both sides are defined. Identities can be used to simplify expressions and prove two forms are equivalent.

Strategies for Proving Trigonometric Identities
Begin with the more complicated side. Convert sec, csc, cot, and tan to sine and cosine when useful. Look for common denominators, factoring, expansion, and the Pythagorean identity. Work on one side until it matches the other; do not assume the identity is already true.
